Six directions, one fix-list

Six ways to fix a 32-year-old business’s website.

WHYNOT Electronics runs 25 showrooms across the Godavari districts and has been trading since 1994. Their current site buries the phone number, breaks every map link and shows a shopping cart with nothing in it. These are six complete redesigns of the same content — different visual language, identical job: make a customer trust them in five seconds, then get them to the nearest showroom or a catalog they can order from.

The brief

Same five problems in all six builds

Every trial carries the full fix-list. The difference is entirely in how they look and feel — which is the point of building more than one.

  1. One true set of facts. The current site claims 23, 25 and 40 branches on three different pages. All six trials say 25 showrooms in 23 towns, and say it once.
  2. A showroom directory that works. Search by town, district or pincode, with real Google Maps links. Every map link on the live site is broken.
  3. A phone number one tap away, on every page, with an honest open/closed state — the live contact page has no number at all.
  4. Products worth browsing. All six trials ship a real catalogue with categories, search, town filters and a shortlist that travels to the counter by WhatsApp, phone or email.
  5. A page that loads in a village. No framework, no CDN, no analytics, no popup. Under 250 KB for the first view, readable before the fonts land.

Trial 01

Paper & Ink — the quiet one

Editorial minimalism. Warm paper, hairline rules, real typography and a single brand blue doing all the shouting. Almost no chrome: the design gets out of the way so the addresses, the prices-on-request and the phone number can do the selling. It is the direction for a company that wants to look like it has nothing to prove.

Trial 02

Crayon & Paper — the loud one

Hand-drawn and vibrant. Marker-pen doodles, sticker buttons with hard offset shadows, wobbly pen borders and a maths-exercise-book dot grid. Every icon is a hand-written SVG path rather than a stock glyph, and the copy talks like a shopkeeper instead of a brochure. This is the direction for a brand that wants to feel like the friendly expert in town rather than a showroom.

Trial 03

Panel & Ink — the anime one

Built like a manga page: ruled panels, hard ink borders, halftone screentones, speed lines and impact bursts, with cel-shaded key art. The discipline that keeps it from turning into a sticker album is that the page is a grid — energy is spent in exactly four places (hero, section titles, price tags, CTA) and everything else stays flat and readable. This is the direction for a brand that wants the energy of a festival-hoarding poster with the structure of a catalogue.

The honest caveat on this one:

The two key visuals here are AI-generated stand-in art. Anime is a style where the craft shows — a commissioned illustrator would give this direction a real face, consistent character design and a house palette, which is exactly what a 25-showroom brand would want on hoardings. This trial proves the layout, the panel system and the catalogue work; the art is the part to buy in.

Trial 4

Slab & Stencil — the neo-brutalist one

No gradients, no soft edges, no photographs. Thick black rules, flat blocks of electric colour, one hard offset shadow on every surface and type that behaves like signage. The hero is a poster of stacked blocks instead of a picture, and the town band is a wall of coloured slabs. It is the design that shouts the loudest and loads the smallest — there is not a single image in it.

Why it works for a 25-branch retailer:

Brutalism reads as confidence and it is unmissable from across a street, which is roughly the job a shopfront does. The honest trade-off is warmth: this is the direction least likely to make anyone feel cosy, and the marquee strip is the only motion on the page for a reason.

Trial 5

Glass & Glow — the glassmorphism one

Frosted sheets floating over a lit backdrop: a light study, a colour mesh, blurred orbs and a dark wash, with 22px of backdrop blur on every surface. Depth comes from light instead of borders. This is the most premium of the six and the hardest to keep readable — which is why it ships with an actual measurement, not an opinion.

The contrast pass, because glass is where accessibility usually dies:

The audit composites the real layer stack — backdrop, wash, sheet, text — and checks WCAG ratios against the worst-case backdrop, a nearly white bloom, not the average one. It failed on first run: faint labels at 3.60:1, body text on the lighter tiles at 4.09:1, and every translucent white chip putting white text on a near-white surface. All 24 combinations now pass, including 6.43:1 for body text in the worst case.

Trial 6

Brush & Bloom — the painted one

Pastel and plein-air: a warm cream ground with a canvas grain, thin washes of blush, sage, sky and butter instead of opaque cards, and three impressionist landscapes sitting in soft frames with their edges feathered into the paper. Brush-stroke ribbons replace the hard rules between sections. If the other trials are graphics, this one is a painting.

The caveat that matters most here:

The three paintings are AI-generated stand-ins. Pastel landscape work is exactly the kind of craft where a commissioned illustrator would raise the ceiling most, and it is worth quoting separately rather than hidden. The design elements themselves — frames, washes, grain, dividers — are all plain CSS and SVG, so the layout stands up the moment real artwork arrives.
